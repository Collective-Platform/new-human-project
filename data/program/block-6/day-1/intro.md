## Why Study Galatians and Colossians Together?

Ephesians introduced Christ’s creation of one new humanity in Ephesians 2:14 to 16, and Galatians and Colossians continue that conversation. Galatians explores how people belong to Christ through faith, receive the Spirit, and live in freedom and love. Colossians focuses on who Christ is and how those raised with him put on the new self and live together.

**Freedom, fullness, and a new humanity**

Galatians describe freedom in Christ expressed through love and the Spirit (5:1, 13–25). Colossians declares Christ’s supremacy and speaks of believers’ fullness in him (1:15–20; 2:9–10). Together they ask what grounds the New Human Project and what sustains its life.

**Belonging without added conditions**

Galatians address circumcision and observance of the law as contested conditions of belonging (2:3–5; 5:2–6). It declares that those who belong to Christ are one in him across Jew/Greek, slave/free, and male/female distinctions (3:26–29). Colossians warn against judgment based on food, festivals, and other practices (2:16–23). Neither letter uses Ephesians’ exact phrase “one new humanity,” but both contribute to the question of how people belong together in Christ.

**New identity becomes shared practice**

Galatians connect faith and freedom with love, walking by the Spirit, and bearing one another’s burdens (5:6, 13–26; 6:2). Colossians explicitly describe putting off the old self and putting on the new self, renewed in the Creator’s image, where ethnic and social divisions no longer define standing in Christ (3:9–11). Compassion, forgiveness, worship, and peace then take shape in community (3:12–17). These are concrete practices of what our church calls A New Humanity.

**The New Human Project remains centred on Christ**

Galatians call believers children of God through faith, belonging to Christ and heirs of the promise (3:26–29). Colossians describe reconciliation through Christ, life with him, and the new self being renewed (1:21–23; 2:12–13; 3:9–11). Read alongside Ephesians, these passages deepen the theme while preserving each letter’s distinct argument.

## Two guiding questions

Galatians: What does the gospel of Christ mean for freedom, belonging, and life by the Spirit?

Colossians: Who is Christ, and how does his supremacy reshape the believer’s identity and daily life?

As you read, distinguish Paul’s own wording from our church’s framing. “A New Humanity” connects these letters to our Ephesians journey; let Galatians and Colossians tell us in their own words how faith, the Spirit, union with Christ, and shared life shape that vision.

## A Word Before You Begin

After studying Ephesians and its vision of one new humanity (Ephesians 2:15), we turn to Galatians and Colossians to learn how we belong to Christ and how life in him takes shape. Galatians ask what the gospel is, who belongs to the kingdom of God, and what freedom looks like. Colossians ask who Christ is and how his supremacy reshapes the believer’s identity and everyday relationships.

This study follows the arguments of the biblical passages rather than treating The New Human Project as a phrase found in Galatians or Colossians. Our church’s theme isn’t explicitly found in every verse throughout Galatians and Colossians, but in verses where Paul speaks about shared identity, the new self, love, and community, we will make the connection explicit; elsewhere we will preserve his particular emphasis.

Both letters possess different directions and motives. Galatians is urgent and confrontational. Paul defends the gospel boldly stating that it is for anyone who believes in Jesus as the Saviour, nothing else. Colossians is written to ground believers in what Paul presents as the fullness and sufficiency of Christ, while connecting that vision to a transformed way of life.

The four movements move from foundation to formation: the gospel of grace; freedom and life by the Spirit; Christ above all; and the new life lived together. Together they explore how the gospel shapes the people we are becoming in Christ — the church-wide vision we call The New Human Project.
